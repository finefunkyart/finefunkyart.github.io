Collection 01

Rochester Premo B

Folding plate camera · Rochester, New York · c. 1894–1902

The Premo B was produced during the remarkable rise of Rochester, New York, as a center of American camera manufacturing. Built in mahogany with polished and lacquered brass fittings, the model offered a central swing back, rising-and-falling front, removable lens board, and reversible finder. It was sold in 4×5 and 5×7 formats for photographers who wanted a camera suitable for both hand and tripod work. This collection example retains its PREMO B nameplate and prominent Victor shutter. Its folding bed, bellows, woodwork, hardware, and age wear preserve the character of a photographic instrument now more than a century old.

Collection 02

Zeiss Ikon Contessa LK

35mm viewfinder camera · West Germany · c. 1962–1966

The Contessa LK belongs to Zeiss Ikon’s refined early-1960s line of 35mm cameras. Made in Stuttgart, it paired a fixed 50mm f/2.8 Tessar lens with a Prontor leaf shutter and a coupled selenium exposure meter. The photographer matched the meter needle while setting shutter speed and aperture manually—a bridge between fully mechanical photography and the increasingly automated cameras that followed. Its metal body, leatherette covering, precision controls, and clean modernist styling are characteristic of postwar West German camera design.

Collection 03

Balda Baldinette

35mm folding camera · West Germany · c. 1950–1955

Produced after World War II, the Baldinette revived Balda’s tradition of compact folding 35mm cameras. Its lens folds into the body behind a hinged front door, allowing a capable full-frame camera to travel in a remarkably small package. The Baldinette reflects the resurgence of West German optical manufacturing in the early 1950s and the era’s fascination with precision cameras that were both technically serious and portable enough for everyday use.

Collection 04

Kodak Brownie 8 Movie Camera

8mm home-movie camera · Brownie movie line introduced 1951 · variant date under study

Kodak introduced its low-priced Brownie 8mm Movie Camera in 1951, bringing motion-picture making within reach of far more families. These spring-driven cameras helped turn birthdays, vacations, holidays, and ordinary domestic life into moving personal archives. Kodak expanded the Brownie movie family throughout the 1950s and early 1960s with several lens and body variations. The exact production year of this collection example will be narrowed further from its specific model markings.

Collection 05

Kodak Brownie Reflex Synchro

127 roll-film camera · U.S. production 1941–1952 · U.K. production to 1960

The Brownie Reflex Synchro used a large brilliant waist-level finder that gave the inexpensive camera the visual presence of a twin-lens reflex, while keeping operation intentionally simple. The synchronized version added connections for flash photography and used 127 roll film. American production ran from 1941 to 1952; British production continued after the war through 1960. It represents Kodak’s long effort to make family photography affordable, approachable, and dependable.

Collection 06

Herco Imperial 620

620 snapshot box camera · Chicago, Illinois · c. 1950s

Made by the Herbert George Company of Chicago, the Herco Imperial was an inexpensive plastic snapshot camera built around 620 roll film. Its fixed-focus simplicity made photography accessible without complicated settings: frame the subject, release the shutter, and advance the film. Herbert George sold closely related cameras with different faceplates and branding, a clever way of reaching different markets while using the same basic design. The bold front plate and molded body make the Imperial an unmistakable artifact of 1950s American consumer design.

Collection 07

Kodak Brownie Bullet

127 roll-film snapshot camera · 1957–1964

The Brownie Bullet was a compact postwar snapshot camera sold during the height of Kodak’s mass-market Brownie era. Produced from 1957 to 1964, it used 127 roll film and emphasized the Brownie philosophy of uncomplicated picture-making. Its molded body, straightforward viewfinder, and minimal controls reflect a period when cameras increasingly became familiar household objects rather than specialized equipment—small machines designed to preserve family life one frame at a time.
