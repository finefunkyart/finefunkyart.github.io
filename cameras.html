Vintage Cameras

A small museum of machines that once held light. These photographs show the actual cameras from Jaqulin Joseph’s collection, presented on a clean gallery-white field.

Collection catalog

Objects & histories

Arranged chronologically from the oldest camera to the newest, the collection traces changing technologies, design, and everyday photographic life across generations.

c. 1894–1902
Collection 01

Rochester Premo B

Folding plate camera · Rochester, New York · c. 1894–1902

The Premo B came from Rochester Optical Company during Rochester’s rise as a major American camera-making center. Built in mahogany with polished metal fittings, bellows, a folding bed, rising-and-falling front and central swing back, it was offered in 4×5 and 5×7 formats. This example retains the PREMO B nameplate and Victor shutter.

At the end of the nineteenth century, cameras like this sat at an important crossroads between the cumbersome studio view camera and the increasingly portable field camera. The folding bed protected the bellows and lens standard when closed, while the front and back movements gave a serious photographer more control over composition and perspective than a simple snapshot camera could offer.

1916–1926
Collection 02

No. 2 Folding Cartridge Premo

120 roll-film folding camera · Eastman Kodak · Rochester, New York · 1916–1926

The No. 2 Folding Cartridge Premo was produced by Eastman Kodak from 1916 to 1926 and used 120 roll film for approximately 2¼ × 3¼-inch photographs. Its compact folding body, leather-covered exterior, bellows and metal front standard made it a practical portable camera during the years when roll-film photography was moving steadily into everyday life.

The Premo name connects this model to Rochester’s earlier camera-making tradition, which Kodak absorbed and carried forward as the company expanded its photographic system for amateur users.

c. 1933–1946
Collection 03

Cine-Kodak Eight Model 20

8mm motion-picture camera · Eastman Kodak · c. 1933–1946

One of Kodak’s compact 8mm movie cameras, the Model 20 belongs to the era when home movies became increasingly practical for families. Its upright body, folding finder and exposure guide supported simple spring-driven filming; this example carries an Anastigmat f/2.7 lens.

Kodak introduced 8mm film in the early 1930s as a more economical alternative to 16mm home-movie film, helping families preserve birthdays, vacations and everyday motion long before consumer video.

1934–1942
Collection 04

Kodak Brownie Junior Six-20

620 roll-film box camera · Eastman Kodak · 1934–1942

The Brownie Junior Six-20 used 620 roll film and wrapped very simple mechanics in a striking geometric faceplate. Twin brilliant finders supported horizontal or vertical framing, while its Art Deco styling made the camera itself an appealing household object.

The Brownie family was central to Kodak’s campaign to put photography into ordinary hands. The Junior Six-20’s decorative face is especially characteristic of the Depression era, when geometry, chrome-like accents and symmetry made even inexpensive products look modern.

1936–1942
Collection 05

Eastman Kodak Bullet

127 roll-film snapshot camera · Eastman Kodak · Rochester, New York · 1936–1942

The Kodak Bullet is a small Streamline Moderne camera designed during the great Art Deco age of American consumer products. Its black Bakelite body, raised horizontal ribs, large circular helical lens housing and folding metal eye-level finder give it a strong sculptural presence despite its simple snapshot mechanics.

Produced from 1936 to 1942, the Bullet used 127 roll film and was designed to make photography inexpensive and uncomplicated. Its molded body is closely associated with industrial designer Walter Dorwin Teague’s streamlined work for Kodak. The telescoping front extends for picture taking and retracts into the body for carrying, while the folding finder disappears against the top when not in use. The result is a camera that is historically important not because it was technically complex, but because it brought modern industrial design and everyday photography together in one memorable object.

Collection 06

Kodak Six-16 Senior

616 roll-film folding camera · Eastman Kodak · 1937–1939

The Kodak Six-16 Senior was a self-erecting folding camera made during the late 1930s for 616 roll film. This example carries a Kodak Anastigmat f/6.3 128mm lens and Kodak-Dakar No. 1 shutter.

Its folding bed, bellows, direct-view finder and metal-trimmed black body place it squarely in Kodak’s transition toward more compact, polished amateur cameras while still preserving the elegant mechanics of the classic folding form.

Falcon Miniature

Miniature snapshot camera · Utility Mfg. Co., New York · c. late 1930s

A compact American snapshot camera made by Utility Manufacturing Company in New York. Its molded black body, simple finder and bold circular faceplate reflect the Art Deco styling of inexpensive cameras from the late 1930s.

Kodak Brownie Reflex Synchro

127 roll-film reflex-style camera · Eastman Kodak · 1941–1960

The Brownie Reflex Synchro used a large brilliant waist-level finder that gave an inexpensive family camera the visual presence of a twin-lens reflex. The synchronized version added flash capability while keeping operation intentionally simple.

Its upright shape recalls professional twin-lens reflex cameras, but Kodak simplified the experience dramatically, giving generations of amateur photographers an inviting way to frame family life.

Produced after World War II, the Brownie Flash Six-20 continued Kodak’s affordable family-camera tradition while adding flash synchronization. It used 620 film and simple distance settings for casual snapshot photography.

Flash synchronization freed family photography from daylight, making birthdays, holiday gatherings and evening events easier to preserve while keeping the familiar Brownie simplicity intact.

Collection 10

Balda Baldinette

35mm folding camera · West Germany · c. 1950–1955

The Baldinette brought Balda’s folding-camera tradition into the 35mm era. Its collapsible lens standard and bellows allowed a capable full-frame camera to fold into a remarkably compact form, reflecting postwar demand for precision and portability.

It represents a final flourishing of the folding camera before rigid-body 35mm rangefinders and single-lens reflex cameras increasingly defined serious portable photography.

620 snapshot box camera · Chicago, Illinois · c. 1950s

Made by the Herbert George Company of Chicago, the Herco Imperial was an inexpensive plastic snapshot camera designed around simplicity. Its bold faceplate and molded body make it an unmistakable artifact of 1950s American consumer-camera design.

Plastic construction lowered costs and encouraged expressive styling, helping photography reach children, vacationing families and casual picture-makers as readily as serious hobbyists.

1959–1963
Collection 14

Zeiss Ikon Contaflex Super

35mm leaf-shutter SLR · Zeiss Ikon · Stuttgart, West Germany · 1959–1963

Introduced in 1959, the original Contaflex Super was one of the more sophisticated cameras in Zeiss Ikon’s postwar Contaflex line. This model paired a Carl Zeiss Tessar 50mm f/2.8 lens with a Synchro-Compur leaf shutter and a coupled selenium exposure meter. The prominent meter cell and front control wheel made it visually distinctive while giving photographers exposure information without requiring a battery.

The Contaflex family occupied an unusual place in 35mm history: it was a true single-lens reflex camera built around a leaf shutter rather than the focal-plane shutter that later became standard for SLRs. On the Super, the lens’s front optical group could be exchanged for Pro-Tessar wide-angle and telephoto components, and interchangeable film backs could allow a photographer to change film types mid-roll. The camera is a dense little machine—precision metalwork, optical engineering and mid-century German design gathered around a lens that still wears the elegant handwritten “Contaflex” name.

c. 1962–1966
Collection 15

Zeiss Ikon Contessa LK

35mm camera · West Germany · c. 1962–1966

The Contessa LK represents Zeiss Ikon’s polished postwar approach to compact 35mm photography. Its metal body, bright finder, selenium-cell light meter and Carl Zeiss lens combine precision German engineering with elegant mid-century design.

By the 1960s, built-in meters were helping photographers move beyond guesswork. A selenium meter generated its own tiny electrical current from incoming light and required no battery, a practical feature that remains part of the camera’s collector appeal.

Collection 16

Polaroid Swinger Model 20

Instant camera · United States · 1965–1970

Introduced in 1965, the Swinger became one of Polaroid’s best-known mass-market cameras. Its light molded body, pop-up viewfinder and inexpensive instant-picture system helped make instant photography youthful, social and accessible.

The Model 20 used black-and-white peel-apart instant film and helped turn photography into an immediate social experience decades before digital cameras and smartphones made instant image-sharing ordinary.
