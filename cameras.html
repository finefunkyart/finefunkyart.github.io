c. 1894–1902
Collection 01

Rochester Premo B

Folding plate camera · Rochester, New York · c. 1894–1902

The Premo B came from Rochester Optical Company during Rochester’s rise as a major American camera-making center. Built in mahogany with polished metal fittings, bellows, a folding bed, rising-and-falling front and central swing back, it was offered in 4×5 and 5×7 formats. This example retains the PREMO B nameplate and Victor shutter.

At the end of the nineteenth century, cameras like this sat at an important crossroads between the cumbersome studio view camera and the increasingly portable field camera. The folding bed protected the bellows and lens standard when closed, while the front and back movements gave a serious photographer more control over composition and perspective than a simple snapshot camera could offer. Rochester Optical was part of the dense photographic industry that made Rochester, New York, synonymous with American camera production. The Premo name would later become associated with Eastman Kodak after corporate consolidation in the early twentieth century, making surviving Premo cameras tangible links to the formative years of the American photographic trade.

c. 1933–1946
Collection 02

Cine-Kodak Eight Model 20

8mm motion-picture camera · Eastman Kodak · c. 1933–1946

One of Kodak’s compact 8mm movie cameras, the Model 20 belongs to the era when home movies became increasingly practical for families. Its upright body, folding finder and exposure guide supported simple spring-driven filming; this example carries an Anastigmat f/2.7 lens.

Kodak introduced 8mm film in the early 1930s as a more economical alternative to 16mm home-movie film. The format used 16mm-width film exposed twice—once in each direction—before processing and splitting it into two 8mm strips. That clever economy made personal motion pictures more attainable. A spring-wound camera such as the Model 20 needed no battery; the photographer wound the mechanism, framed the scene and let the motor pull film past the lens. Cameras like this preserved birthdays, vacations, children at play and neighborhood life, creating moving family archives long before consumer video. Their survival reminds us that the desire to record everyday motion predates the digital era by generations.

1934–1942
Collection 03

Kodak Brownie Junior Six-20

620 roll-film box camera · Eastman Kodak · 1934–1942

The Brownie Junior Six-20 used 620 roll film and wrapped very simple mechanics in a striking geometric faceplate. Twin brilliant finders supported horizontal or vertical framing, while its Art Deco styling made the camera itself an appealing household object.

The Brownie family was central to Kodak’s campaign to put photography into ordinary hands. By the 1930s, the camera was no longer merely a technical apparatus; it was becoming part of vacations, holidays, school days and family gatherings. The Six-20 designation refers to Kodak’s 620 film spool, introduced in the early 1930s. Although the film itself was essentially the same width as 120 film, the slimmer spool allowed manufacturers to build more compact cameras. The Junior Six-20’s decorative front is especially characteristic of the period: industrial designers used geometry, chrome-like accents and symmetry to make inexpensive products look modern. The result is both a photographic tool and a small piece of Depression-era design history.

c. late 1930s
Collection 04

Falcon Miniature

Miniature snapshot camera · Utility Mfg. Co., New York · c. late 1930s

A compact American snapshot camera made by Utility Manufacturing Company in New York. Its molded black body, simple finder and bold circular faceplate reflect the Art Deco styling of inexpensive cameras from the late 1930s.

Cameras in this category were built for a rapidly expanding amateur market. Rather than asking the owner to understand plates, focusing screens or complicated shutter settings, manufacturers reduced photography to a few basic actions: point, frame and expose. Molded bodies lowered production costs and opened the door to strong decorative styling, which is why the Falcon’s face carries so much visual personality despite its simple mechanics. Small American cameras of the Depression era also tell a social history: photography was becoming less exclusively the territory of professionals and affluent hobbyists and more a part of ordinary family life. Their modest technology is precisely what made them culturally powerful.

1941–1960
Collection 05

Kodak Brownie Reflex Synchro

127 roll-film reflex-style camera · 1941–1960

The Brownie Reflex Synchro used a large brilliant waist-level finder that gave an inexpensive family camera the visual presence of a twin-lens reflex. The synchronized version added flash capability while keeping operation intentionally simple.

Its upright box shape recalls professional twin-lens reflex cameras, but the Brownie simplified the experience dramatically. Looking down into the finder was itself part of the pleasure: the photographer saw a bright miniature scene floating inside the camera before pressing the shutter. Flash synchronization broadened the camera’s usefulness indoors and at night, important as flashbulb photography became a familiar part of postwar celebrations and social events. The Reflex demonstrates one of Kodak’s recurring talents—borrowing the visual language of more sophisticated equipment while removing enough complexity to make the result approachable for millions of amateur photographers.

1946–1955
Collection 06

Kodak Brownie Flash Six-20

620 roll-film box camera · Eastman Kodak · 1946–1955

Produced after World War II, the Brownie Flash Six-20 continued Kodak’s affordable family-camera tradition while adding flash synchronization. It used 620 film and a simple two-position focus system marked for roughly 5–10 feet and beyond 10 feet.

The addition of synchronized flash mattered because it freed snapshot photography from daylight. Families could photograph birthdays in the dining room, evening parties and holiday gatherings with far more confidence. The camera still demanded very little technical knowledge, which was central to the Brownie philosophy established decades earlier: Kodak supplied the complicated photographic ecosystem while the owner concentrated on the moment. Its straightforward distance settings are a good example of that philosophy. Instead of precise focusing, the photographer selected a broad distance range. The Flash Six-20 therefore marks a period when photographic convenience was expanding without abandoning the sturdy box-camera form familiar to generations of users.

c. 1950–1955
Collection 07

Balda Baldinette

35mm folding camera · West Germany · c. 1950–1955

The Baldinette brought Balda’s folding-camera tradition into the 35mm era. Its collapsible lens standard and bellows allowed a capable full-frame camera to fold into a remarkably compact form, reflecting postwar demand for precision and portability.

After World War II, West German camera manufacturers rebuilt an industry already respected for fine optics and mechanical craftsmanship. The Baldinette belongs to the transitional moment when folding construction—long associated with larger roll-film cameras—was adapted to the increasingly dominant 35mm format. When closed, the lens assembly retreated into the body, making the camera easier to carry; when opened, the bellows extended the lens to its working position. This marriage of old and new technology gives the Baldinette much of its charm. It represents a final flourishing of the folding camera before rigid-body 35mm rangefinders and single-lens reflex cameras increasingly defined serious portable photography.

c. 1950s
Collection 08

Herco Imperial 620

620 snapshot box camera · Chicago, Illinois · c. 1950s

Made by the Herbert George Company of Chicago, the Herco Imperial was an inexpensive plastic snapshot camera designed around simplicity. Its bold faceplate and molded body make it an unmistakable artifact of 1950s American consumer-camera design.

Herbert George and similar manufacturers occupied a different corner of the market from premium German and Japanese camera makers. Their cameras were affordable, visually lively and often sold as accessible gifts or first cameras. Plastic construction was not simply a compromise; it was part of a larger transformation in American manufacturing, allowing objects to be formed quickly into shapes and textures that would have been expensive in metal. The Imperial 620 belongs to the democratizing side of photographic history, where the goal was not laboratory precision but participation. Cameras like this helped ensure that photography could belong to a child, a vacationing family or a casual picture-maker just as readily as to a professional.

1950s
Collection 09

Kodak Brownie 8 Movie Camera

8mm home-movie camera · Kodak Brownie movie line · 1950s

Kodak’s low-priced Brownie 8mm movie cameras helped turn birthdays, vacations and ordinary family life into moving personal archives. The line expanded through the 1950s with several body and lens variations.

The Brownie name had already taught generations that still photography could be uncomplicated, and Kodak applied the same idea to motion pictures. Eight-millimeter film made home movies substantially less expensive than larger formats, while spring-driven mechanisms kept cameras independent of batteries. Owners typically filmed short silent sequences, then projected the developed reels at home. That ritual—camera, processed film, projector and family audience—became a defining form of twentieth-century domestic memory. A Brownie movie camera is therefore more than an obsolete machine: it represents an era when moving images first became something ordinary people could make about themselves.

1957–1964
Collection 10

Kodak Brownie Bullet

127 roll-film snapshot camera · Eastman Kodak · 1957–1964

The Brownie Bullet was a compact postwar snapshot camera from Kodak’s mass-market Brownie era. Its molded body, simple finder and minimal controls reflect the period when cameras became familiar household objects rather than specialized equipment.

By the late 1950s, Kodak had spent decades teaching consumers that photography could be easy. The Bullet continued that tradition at a moment when plastics were reshaping industrial design and cameras were becoming lighter, cheaper and visually friendlier. Its 127 roll film produced relatively small negatives but was convenient for casual use. There is little pretense of complexity here: the object is a reminder that the history of photography was not driven only by expensive lenses and professional innovation. It was also driven by simple cameras that persuaded people to carry a camera at all. In that sense, the Brownie Bullet belongs directly to the lineage that eventually produced disposable cameras, point-and-shoots and the always-present camera phone.

c. late 1950s–early 1960s
Collection 11

Imperial Satellite 127

127 roll-film snapshot camera · United States · c. late 1950s–early 1960s

With its turquoise body, metallic faceplate and Space Age name, this Imperial camera turns a simple snapshot machine into a small piece of mid-century graphic design. It used 127 roll film and offered straightforward controls for casual family photography.

The name “Satellite” places the camera squarely in the cultural atmosphere of the Space Age. During the late 1950s and early 1960s, rockets, satellites, atomic motifs and futuristic forms appeared across American product design, from automobiles to kitchen appliances. Camera manufacturers joined in. The bright molded body and gleaming front trim gave an inexpensive photographic device the excitement of new technology even though its underlying mechanics remained uncomplicated. That contrast is part of its historical appeal: the Satellite records not only photographs but an era’s optimism about science, plastics, color and the future.

c. 1962–1966
Collection 12

Zeiss Ikon Contessa LK

35mm camera · West Germany · c. 1962–1966

The Contessa LK represents Zeiss Ikon’s polished postwar approach to compact 35mm photography. Its metal body, bright finder, selenium-cell light meter and Carl Zeiss lens combine precision German engineering with elegant mid-century design.

By the 1960s, built-in exposure meters were helping photographers move beyond guesswork and handheld meters. A selenium meter generated its own tiny electrical current from incoming light and therefore required no battery, a practical advantage that still interests collectors today. Zeiss Ikon drew on a long German tradition of optical and mechanical engineering, and cameras such as the Contessa occupied a refined middle ground: compact enough for travel, yet substantially more sophisticated than a fixed-focus snapshot camera. The clean metal-and-black finish also reflects a changing idea of what a modern camera should look like—less decorative than many 1930s models and increasingly defined by purposeful controls, optical quality and precision.

1965–1970
Collection 13

Polaroid Swinger Model 20

Instant camera · United States · 1965–1970

Introduced in 1965, the Swinger became one of Polaroid’s best-known mass-market cameras. Its light molded body, pop-up viewfinder and inexpensive instant-picture system helped make instant photography youthful, social and accessible.

The Swinger arrived when instant photography was shifting from a technical marvel into popular culture. Polaroid deliberately marketed the camera to younger buyers, and its low price, compact body and famous advertising helped make the act of watching a photograph appear almost as important as taking it. The Model 20 used black-and-white peel-apart instant film and included a simple exposure aid that told the photographer when the available light was sufficient. It was not designed as a precision professional instrument; its importance lies in something broader. The Swinger helped turn photography into an immediate social experience decades before digital cameras and smartphones made instant image-sharing ordinary.
